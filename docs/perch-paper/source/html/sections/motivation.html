A player's Nido wallet holds a Perch account. The player opens Guess the Number, a Soroban game that takes 1 XLM for each wrong guess and pays out its balance on a right one, and wants the game to play without a passkey prompt on every turn. A useful permission says which contract may be called, which function may run, which signer may act, how much may be spent over time, and when the permission ends. It does not give the game the owner's ability to replace credentials or configure recovery.

The paper follows the grants that the experiment in §10 installs. Grant 1, the demo's “original app”, may transfer at most 10 XLM in any 100-ledger window, about eight minutes at the nominal five-second ledger: a 6-XLM transfer succeeds, and a further 5 XLM in that window must fail. Grant 2 gives that app a fresh key after recovery. Grant 3 lets the game call guess and transfer at most 2 XLM per 100 ledgers, so two lost guesses fit and a third is refused. A grant derived from another, for example one an agent hands to a sub-agent (§13), must allow nothing the original does not: no split call, no recipient outside its conditions, no route to the account through another contract. If the owner loses a key, a completed recovery must end the old grants' authority even before their storage is collected.

Perch makes this decision a pure state transition and separates what is proven about it from the obligations of the account that invokes it.

Perch in one picture

Figure 1 shows the parts. The rule engine, rule_eval, is a library compiled into the account contract, and every authorization record lives in the account's storage. The account calls verifiers on every authorization, the document compiler on every install, the recovery controller on account-document writes, upgrades, cancellations and completions, the zero-knowledge pool on enrollment, and any contract through an owner-authorized execute. It derives each infrastructure address under the current network ID from a Wasm hash and registry path fixed at build time, so a later registry publication cannot change the code an existing account calls; it reaches a new compiler only through its own delayed upgrade.

Soroban's authorization tree

On Soroban, a contract calls require_auth when it needs an address's authorization, and a custom account implements __check_auth. For each authorization entry, the host passes the account a signature payload and the entry's contexts: the contract, function and arguments of every call in its invocation tree, flattened in preorder, root first. The payload hashes the network ID, a nonce the host consumes, a signature-expiration ledger and the invocation tree, so the host stops an entry from being replayed on the same account; §06 adds the binding across accounts. The account must decide every context; approving the root alone would leave the rest unchecked. Stellar's custom-account example describes this interface.

As in OpenZeppelin's Soroban account, the signer names one installed rule per context in the authorization payload (context_rule_ids). The account resolves those IDs to stored rules, passes them to the engine and verifies signatures afterwards (§06). Figure 2 shows the game's authorizations from the account's side.
