ABSTRACT

Perch lets the owner of a Stellar smart account give an app or agent a grant: a bounded permission naming the contracts it may call, whom it may pay, how much it may spend in any window of ledgers, and until when. The account checks every call made in its name against the grant at one decision point, and keeps the spending record in its own storage. The deciding function is translated into the Lean proof assistant, where machine-checked proofs show that it accepts only what the rules allow and that no capped rule exceeds its limit within a window; the contract around it is tested, not proved, and not yet audited. In this page, soroban-sim runs the real contract code of Perch and of an OpenZeppelin account with oz-policy-builder side by side, offline: you choose the payments and see where each design decides. Perch's design is unmerged and runs on testnet as a preview.

At a glance

  • Bounded over time. A cap holds in any window of ledgers, charged in the same decision that checks the call (Figure 2, Theorem 6).
  • One decision point. The account itself decides every call an app makes in its name, and nothing is written unless the signature also checks (Figure 3).
  • Proved core. The decision function's Lean translation is proved to match a model and keep these bounds; the code around it is tested, and nothing is audited (§08).
  • Testable end to end. soroban-sim runs Perch and an OpenZeppelin account with oz-policy-builder side by side in this page, offline (§07).

01What can an app do with a grant?

A grant lets an app or agent act for an account within limits the account itself enforces.

On Stellar, a smart account is a contract that holds a user's funds and decides which calls may act in its name; Soroban is the platform that runs such contracts. An app that spends for a user today has two choices: ask the user to approve every call, or hold a key that can do anything the account can. Neither suits an agent that pays a service many times an hour. Perch adds a third. The owner installs a grant, a written and bounded permission, and the account checks against it every call the app makes in the account's name. The overview diagram shows who does what.

The shop grant

The running example lets an app send XLM, Stellar's native currency, from the account to one shop, at most 10 XLM in any 100 ledgers (Figure 1). A ledger is one closed block of the network, nominally 5 seconds apart, so 100 ledgers is about eight minutes. The grant has one rule. It names the contract the app may call (the XLM contract), the function (transfer), a condition on the recipient, the key that must sign (the app's), and a cap: a limit on the total sent in a sliding window of ledgers. The simulation in §07 installs this grant.

What a grant cannot say

  • A bound on one call's amount. The cap bounds the total in the window, so one call may spend the whole limit.
  • Or and not. Every condition in a rule must hold.
  • Reads of other contracts, such as a price oracle.
  • How calls relate. The account checks each call alone, so a rule meant for a payment a game makes on the user's behalf also allows the same payment made directly (§02, the game-grant diagram).

The claim, word by word

This paper argues one claim: safe, bounded delegation for apps and agents on Stellar, simple, proved, and testable end to end in soroban-sim. Each word has a narrow meaning here.

  • Safe and bounded mean three properties, proved for the decision function and for lowering, with the account code around them tested; they do not mean audited or ready for real funds. Each call of an accepted request is allowed by some installed rule (Theorem 2); no window of a capped rule holds more than its limit (Theorem 6); and no grant allows a call to the account itself or its adopted recovery contracts (Theorem 8).
  • Simple means one document, one install call (apply_scope) and one decision point. The cost is fewer conditions than oz-policy-builder, a rule builder for OpenZeppelin's Soroban accounts, offers (§06).
  • Proved applies to the translation of the decision function into Lean, a proof assistant whose kernel checks every proof. It does not apply to the compiled contract; the code around the function is tested (§08).
  • End to end covers the contract code of both designs, run together in soroban-sim, a portable Soroban host: Perch's account, verifier and document compiler; OpenZeppelin's account and spending_limit; oz-policy-builder's interpreter; and the XLM contract. Wallets, RPC servers, passkeys and fees are outside it.

Grants also end with the account's recovery: if the owner recovers the account after losing a key, one counter moves and every earlier grant stops authorizing through the account in that transaction (the vision paper).

02How does a Soroban account approve a call?

On Soroban the account itself approves every call made in its name, and it decides each call alone.

How Soroban asks an account

A Soroban contract that needs an address's approval calls require_auth on it. When the address is a contract account, the host, the program that runs contracts for the network, calls that account's __check_auth [3, 4]. So in the shop grant the app never calls the account: it calls the XLM contract's transfer(from, to, amount) with the account as from, and transfer's require_auth makes the host ask the account (the recorded-run figure shows this order in its traces). A transaction carries one authorization entry for each address whose approval it needs. For each entry the host passes __check_auth three things: the payload hash, which signatures cover; an auth payload, whose shape the account defines (here the signatures and context_rule_ids, one chosen rule per context); and the entry's contexts: every call and contract creation in its invocation tree, in preorder (each call before the calls it makes). The account must decide every context. The host consumes the entry's nonce, a one-time number that stops replay, only when the transaction succeeds, so a refused entry can be resubmitted until it expires.

A signer whose key the account checks itself, through a verifier contract, is an external signer. A delegated signer is another address that approves through the host; CAP-0071 [5], a Core Advancement Proposal, lets that approval ride in the account's own entry. In OpenZeppelin v0.8.0 a delegated signer instead approves in an entry of its own, which also binds the rule IDs. Wallets simulate a transaction before signing it, but recording simulation, the mode that discovers which entries a transaction needs, does not call __check_auth, so it cannot predict a refusal. Enforcing simulation, which runs __check_auth, can.

OpenZeppelin's account

OpenZeppelin's Soroban account library [6] gives both designs their vocabulary. A context rule is its unit of permission: a scope (for example one contract), an optional expiry, signers and policies. The auth payload names one rule per context in context_rule_ids, and signers are External or Delegated, as above. A policy is a separate contract attached to a rule; its enforce hook runs on each call the rule approves. In v0.8.0 the account validates the selected rules, authenticates the signers, and then calls each policy's enforce (storage.rs:629-690).

oz-policy-builder

oz-policy-builder [7] writes rules for OpenZeppelin accounts and has two halves (README.md:13-24). Off chain, a synthesiser records a transaction or takes a declared intent and writes a predicate, a declarative condition on one call. On chain, a shared policy-interpreter contract, attached to a context rule as a policy, evaluates the predicate on every guarded call. The interpreter decides each call alone and keeps no totals; limits over time come from a token allowance with an expiry, or from OpenZeppelin's spending_limit policy beside the interpreter (docs/stellar.md:404-407).

One authorization, several contexts

The game-grant diagram shows one authorization from the account's side, using a game grant. The app may call a game's guess and transfer at most 2 XLM per 100 ledgers to any recipient, since the grant's transfer rule pins none. A lost guess produces two contexts, the game's guess and the transfer the game makes from the account, and the auth payload names one rule for each. A win authorizes the guess alone, because the game pays from its own balance. Because each context is checked alone, a direct transfer within the cap, to the game or to anyone else, is also allowed.

03What can a grant say?

A grant is a set of rules, each naming one contract, the functions and arguments it allows, who signs, when it ends and how much it may spend in a sliding window.

A grant document and an installed grant

A grant document is the JSON the owner submits; Figure 1 shows one. The owner installs it by calling the account's apply_scope (the code calls a grant a scope). The account passes the JSON to the document compiler, a stateless contract that parses and validates it. The account then lowers the result into stored rules with lower_scoped from rule-core, the library that holds Perch's decision function [8]. It lowers under its own address; its grant generation, a counter that only a completed recovery advances; and its excluded set, its adopted recovery contracts: the recovery controller it has adopted and, when its recovery uses a zero-knowledge proof, that factor's adapter and pool (the vision paper). Lowering refuses any rule whose target is the account itself or one of these (Theorem 8; lib.rs:1218-1240, 1328-1345). What it stores is an installed grant: the rules the account consults on every call. The full install path, with its seven steps, canonical bytes and document hash, is in the vision paper.

What a rule checks

A rule allows a call when four conditions hold: the called contract is the rule's rule target; the function is in the rule's list, or the rule lists none; every argument condition holds; and enough of the rule's signers signed to meet its threshold, counted over distinct signers. A document can write five argument conditions, is-self, address-eq, string-in, string-prefix, u32-eq (doc.rs:281-304), and all of a rule's conditions must hold. A rule may end at an expiry: not-after-ledger N authorizes through N − 1. It may carry a cap only when its functions are exactly ["transfer"], and the cap charges the transfer's amount.

Selection is explicit. The auth payload names one rule for each context, and that rule must itself allow the context; the account does not search for another. A signer that belongs to no chosen rule is refused, not ignored.

The spending window

A cap is a limit λ\lambda and a period PP in ledgers. The window at ledger LL holds the charges made at ledgers tt with L−P<t≤LL - P < t \le L. A new charge is accepted when the window's total plus the charge stays at or below λ\lambda, and the account keeps these charges in a spending record in its own storage. The check is part of the decision that checks the call, so there is no separate step to skip. Contexts in one authorization that select the same rule each see the earlier charges in that batch, and a refusal changes nothing.

Figure 2 plays the shop grant against this definition. With JavaScript it is an explorable: set λ\lambda and PP, send amounts, advance the ledger, and compare with checking each call alone. Its "Replace the rule" toggle shows what happens when the owner replaces a capped rule: the new rule starts with an empty window. Theorem 6 states the bound: from empty records, no window holds more than λ\lambda. On chain a spending record lives in one ledger entry, which holds at most 907 spends (the model allows 1,000; the 65,536-byte entry limit binds first), so further transfers through that rule fail until older ones leave the window.

Handing part of a grant to a helper (modelled; not installable at this build)

An app may want to pass part of its grant to a helper, such as a sub-agent. Attenuation derives a narrower grant from an existing one. rule-core defines 10 attenuation steps, among them dropping a rule, removing functions, adding an argument condition, raising a threshold, lowering a cap's limit, lengthening its period and moving the expiry earlier; none changes a rule's target. Theorem 4 proves that a derived grant allows no call its parent does not; caps and expiry do not enter that comparison. Theorem 5, in What Perch proves, covers them: any run the child accepts from empty records, the parent accepts from its own. Each keeps its own records, so neither theorem bounds what a parent and a child spend together. Both are proved for the model and its translation. The account has no entry point that installs an attenuated grant at the pinned build; one is proposed (the vision paper).

Ending a grant

The grant-endings table lists the three ways a grant stops: its expiry, one owner call to remove_scope, or a completed recovery. remove_scope deletes the grant's rules and spending records at once. 04How does the account decide?

The account decides each request at one point, and writes nothing unless the signature also checks.

Figure 3 follows one request through a Perch account in six stages. (1) The host calls __check_auth with the auth payload, which names a rule for each context and the claimed signers. (2) Guards refuse what the account never allows through a grant, such as a grant rule on its adopted recovery contracts. (3) The account looks up each named rule and its spending record. (4) The decision function checks the request against the rules and computes the next spending records. (5) The signatures are checked: a verifier contract checks each external signer, and the host checks a delegated one. (6) The account stores the changed records, and the request is authorized. A refusal at any stage writes nothing. The verifier runs after the decision, so a request the grant refuses never reaches it.

With JavaScript the figure lets you choose a request (a lost guess, a transfer within or over the cap, a call to the account), a signer, the grant's state and the rule named, and lights each stage in turn. When the decision accepts, it names the rule that allows each call: the witness that Theorem 2 promises.

The decision function

Stage 4 is rule_eval::authorize, the evaluator in rule-core cbabe59. It takes the selected rules, their spending records, the contexts, the rule chosen for each context, the claimed signers, the ledger and the grant generation, and returns the next spending records or a Refusal. It reads no storage and checks no signature; the account code around it does both. The theorems in §08 are about this function.

A refusal by the decision function reaches the caller as one of two codes inside Error(Auth, InvalidAction): auth error 3 for a grant issued before a completed recovery, and 5 for every other refusal. The guards and the signature check have the other exits that Figure 3 lists.

What a signature binds

An external signer signs a digest that binds this account and the selected rule IDs, so a relayer cannot move the signature to another rule the signer belongs to, such as an uncapped one. A delegated signer approves the host's payload, which names the account but not the rule IDs. OpenZeppelin v0.8.0 binds the rule IDs for both kinds, so on the delegated path it binds more than Perch.

For wallet and app developers

  • Simulate twice. Recording simulation does not call __check_auth, so it cannot predict a Perch refusal. Sign the entry with the selected rule IDs, then simulate again with enforcing authorization to learn the verdict and the final footprint.
  • Choose the rules. The auth payload names one installed rule per context in context_rule_ids, in context order.
  • Build documents. The npm package @stellar-registry/perch (0.4.1 at the pin; source in packages/perch-js) builds validated grant documents and authorizations, and its requestToPolicyDoc turns an ERC-7715-shaped permission request [9] into a grant document (request.ts:1-11, 49). docs/schema.md describes a JSON Schema for documents.
  • Read refusals. A refusal by the decision function reports auth error 3 or 5 and nothing more; re-run the evaluator off chain to learn which context failed. Guards and signature checks have the codes in Figure 3.
  • Try it. §07 runs the account in this page; the Nido preview creates accounts on testnet.

05What can a rogue or stolen app key do?

A stolen app key can do only what the rules naming it allow, at most each cap's limit per window, and no grant allows a call to the account itself or its adopted recovery contracts; token allowances and the owner's execute lie outside any grant.

We consider two adversaries (Table 1). A1 is the grantee: the app or agent key, or whoever steals it. It can sign anything within the rules that name its key, build any call tree, and choose among those rules. A2 submits or relays transactions: it wraps signed entries, chooses timing and fee, and can resubmit a refused entry until it expires. Below, proved means a Lean theorem checked by Lean's kernel, and tested means tests of the built contracts.

Goals

  • G1 Containment. No grant rule allows a call to the account or its adopted recovery contracts. Proved for lowering (Theorem 8); the account's recheck at authorization time is tested.
  • G2 Permission soundness. An accepted request is allowed by the rules it names. Proved for the decision function (Theorem 2); signer authentication, rule selection and context conversion are tested (O1, O2, O7).
  • G3 Window safety. A capped rule's charges in any window stay within its limit. Proved from empty records (Theorem 6); rule selection and record persistence are tested.
  • G6 Fail-closed refusal. A refusal writes nothing. Tested; in the model a refusal yields no next state.

G4, recovery invalidation, is proved in an account model (Theorem 10), tested and run on testnet; it is set out in the vision paper. G5, signature binding, is tested and has a paper proof in the verification paper.

Trusted: the Soroban host and validators; the verifier contracts that documents name; the adopted recovery controller; the document compiler; rustc; the wallet's display of what is signed; and, for the proofs, Lean's kernel, the Charon and Aeneas translators, and the specification.

Assumed: that the signature schemes the verifiers check (Ed25519, and ECDSA P-256 for passkeys) are unforgeable and that SHA-256 is collision resistant; signatures rest on these assumptions, not on proofs. Under them, the verification paper proves on paper that an external signature binds the account, the entry and the selected rule IDs.

Not goals: budgets shared across grants; effects outside authorization, such as token allowances, the owner's execute and funds held by other contracts; confidentiality; the correctness of verifiers; and what a wallet displays. Theft of the owner's key and the components the account trusts (A3–A5) are covered in the vision paper.

06How does Perch differ from oz-policy-builder?

oz-policy-builder composes a decision from separate contracts; Perch makes it at one point. Each arrangement buys something the other gives up.

This section compares Perch with oz-policy-builder [7] at 43b13ed, on OpenZeppelin v0.8.0, from its public documentation and source. It is a trade-off, not a contest.

Shared ground

Both build on OpenZeppelin's context-rule model and its context_rule_ids field. Both judge each call alone: oz-policy-builder's interpreter "sees one authorised call at a time" (docs/architecture.md:307-309), and so does a Perch rule (the game-grant diagram, case c). Both deny by default.

Two arrangements

An OpenZeppelin account with oz-policy-builder composes its decision from contracts that can be swapped and audited separately: the account's own checks, then the interpreter, spending_limit and any threshold policy attached to the rule. Its README calls the interpreter "one audit-once engine": every policy is data fed into it, so a new combination of constraints needs no new contract and no re-audit (README.md:13-24). Perch puts one document behind one decision point in the account. Composition buys flexibility; one decision point gives one place to check. The composition explorable models the same five checks both ways.

With JavaScript you can attach, detach and reorder the policies in (b) and send the same requests through both arrangements. A live read, such as an oracle price, and a bound on one call's amount fit only (b): a Perch rule reads nothing outside the account, and its cap bounds a window's total, not a single call.

A limit over time

oz-policy-builder's interpreter judges each call and keeps no totals. Its documentation says that amount limits over time come from a token allowance and its expiry, or from OpenZeppelin's spending_limit beside the interpreter (docs/stellar.md:404-407). spending_limit is a separate, opt-in policy that refuses every function but transfer, so the synthesiser installs it only on a rule pinned to transfer (install.ts:957-967). In Perch the window is part of the rule and is charged in the same decision (Theorem 6). §07 runs both designs with and without spending_limit.

Where each is stronger

oz-policy-builder

  • Maturity. Its interpreter is pinned on testnet; it is under external audit, was hardened after a penetration test, and is exercised on recorded mainnet transactions (Table 2, first three rows). Perch's design is unmerged, a testnet preview, and unaudited.
  • Authoring. Record a transaction or declare an intent; synthesise a predicate that permits it and test that each conjunct is needed; verify it against the deployed interpreter's check; review; install unsigned. A CLI and an MCP server expose the same nine tools to shells and agents. A Perch author writes the grant document.
  • Expressiveness and ecosystem fit. Or, comparisons, quantifiers over lists, ledger-clock windows, slippage floors (a minimum a swap must return), reads of other contracts, and calls of one batch related through its execution adapter; OpenZeppelin's threshold policies and other add-ons; a deny code for each reason; rule IDs bound for delegated signers. It works on existing OpenZeppelin accounts: its installer signs for the 0.7.2-line account build already in use on mainnet and for v0.8.0 (policy-synth/README.md:143; deployments/oz-accounts.json:2), while a Perch grant needs a Perch account.

Perch

  • Limits over time. The window is part of the rule's decision, not an optional policy beside it. Caveat: the bound is proved for the decision function's translation, from empty records; rule selection and storage are tested.
  • One decision point. One contract holds the rules and their state, decides before any verifier runs, installs one document across several contracts, and accepts passkey signers on grant rules. Caveat: the account code around the decision is tested, not proved, and nothing is audited.
  • A proved core. Theorems 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 hold for the Lean translation of the decision function (§08). Caveat: the compiled contract is not proved, and nothing is audited.

Lessons for Perch

We propose five things to take from oz-policy-builder:

  1. Recording as authoring. A front end that records a transaction and emits a grant document for the owner to review.
  2. Simulate before install. Run the account's own evaluator in enforcing simulation before a grant is installed, as oz-policy-builder verifies a predicate against the deployed check.
  3. Minimality and overlap checks. Flag rules wider than the recording they came from, and refuse a capped rule that a key it names could bypass through another rule, since a Perch key holds the union of every rule naming it; oz-policy-builder's installer refuses such overlaps (policy-synth/README.md:171).
  4. A corpus of real transactions. Run recorded mainnet transactions through Perch's lowering and evaluator.
  5. An audit before claiming maturity. An external audit before Perch holds real funds.

This section reads both designs from source and runs one task; it measures no costs. The companion paper Perch and oz-policy-builder sets out every dimension with sources, the recorded walkthrough and one intent spelled both ways, with fuller accounts of where oz-policy-builder is stronger, where Perch is and the lessons.

07Run both accounts

soroban-sim runs the real contract code of both designs in this page, offline; you choose the requests.

soroban-sim [10] is a portable Soroban host: the host library the network runs (soroban-env-host 29.0.2, here at protocol 29; testnet now runs protocol 30), packaged so that a program or a web page can run contracts without a network. The simulation keeps one session per design and applies each request with enforcing authorization, the mode in which the host calls __check_auth. Its host is soroban-env-host built with the recording_mode feature, not the exact-metering build soroban-sim also offers, so costs are not exact. Each session holds:

  • for OpenZeppelin, the v0.8.0 account, oz-policy-builder's policy-interpreter, and OpenZeppelin's spending_limit;
  • for Perch, the account, its document compiler and its Ed25519 verifier;
  • in both, the native XLM contract.

The contracts are built from the pinned sources. The OpenZeppelin account, spending_limit and interpreter have the bytes oz-policy-builder records on testnet; the Perch account is the pinned build, not the testnet preview's. Appendix A gives every hash. Both accounts install the shop grant's rule. On the OpenZeppelin side it is the predicate oz-policy-builder's synthesiser writes from our intent, which pins the recipient and bounds each transfer at 10 XLM, with spending_limit at 10 XLM per 100 ledgers beside it. On the Perch side it is one grant with the recipient pinned and the same cap.

For each request the page shows the verdict and code; the authorization trace, from the host's diagnostic events; the authorization entries; the window; a fingerprint of the ledger entries, which a refusal leaves unchanged (a refusal is never submitted); and whether the reply matches the recorded native run.

The signers differ, by design. The OpenZeppelin side signs as a delegated Stellar account, because the interpreter refuses an external signer on the rules it polices (error 212); that is why its requests carry a second authorization entry, in which the delegated signer approves. The Perch side signs with an Ed25519 key that a verifier contract checks. This is not a cost comparison, and the page shows no instruction counts.

Both accounts hold the same rule: the app key may send XLM only to the shop, at most 10 XLM in any 100 ledgers (on the OpenZeppelin side, through spending_limit).

08What is proved?

The function that decides is translated into Lean and proved about; the code around it is tested. Theorem numbers are shared with the verification paper, which lists all thirteen.

What is proved, in five lines

  1. Theorem 1: the Lean translation returns what the model returns, on every input, without panicking or looping.
  2. Theorem 2: when the decision function accepts, each call is allowed by some rule it was given.
  3. Theorem 4: attenuation never widens the calls a grant allows (modelled; not installable yet).
  4. Theorem 6: from an empty record, no window of PP ledgers holds more than the limit.
  5. Theorem 8: no installed grant, nor one derived from it, allows a call to the account or its adopted recovery contracts.

Not covered: the compiled Wasm, the account code around the function (tested), the host, and effects outside authorization. There is no external audit.

The evaluator and its translation

The evaluator is about 2,400 lines of no_std Rust, written in a subset that the Aeneas toolchain can translate. Charon [11] reads the compiler's mid-level representation (MIR) from rustc nightly-2026-09-17, and Aeneas [12] translates it into a pure Lean [13] program; theorems relate that translation to a readable model written by hand. rule-core's CI regenerates the translation and fails on any difference, and checks that its 940 exported theorems use no axioms beyond Lean's standard three. Proved in this paper means checked by Lean's kernel.

09What did running it show?

In the recorded dual run the two designs agreed on every request but three, and a wallet preview ran Perch on Stellar testnet.

Does the account enforce what the design says?

Perch's tests run 57 document traces, generated from rule-core's Lean model, through the natively compiled account and compare every verdict and spending record with the model. In the recorded run of §07, each Perch verdict is the one the shop grant specifies. The two designs gave the same verdict on every request except the three payments made after the owner removed spending_limit, leaving the per-call bound alone. A native run, a WASI build and this page give the same replies.

On Stellar testnet

On 5 November 2026, a scripted browser run of the Nido wallet's preview, with virtual passkeys, made 11 successful testnet transactions. It installed the preview's game grant (two rules; the transfer rule pays only the game, at most 2 XLM per 17,280 ledgers) and made two lost guesses. The wallet declined a third guess before signing because it would exceed the cap, so that refusal was the wallet's, not the chain's. The run also took a one-guardian recovery to completion. The game ran from a development branch with an uncommitted patch.

What does it cost? Perch alone

On the testnet preview, an XLM transfer through a grant rule, signed by a passkey, paid a fee of 12,889 stroops (10,000,000 stroops make one XLM), WebAuthn verification included. A fresh preview account holding the game's grant, after two capped transfers, stores 3,308 bytes and pays 1.677 XLM a year in rent at the network's floor rate. These are Perch's figures alone; we compare no costs across designs.

One task, one host version without exact metering, different signer kinds in the two designs, periodic testnet resets and the patched game all limit what these results show.

10What does Perch not protect?

The proofs cover the decision function's translation, and a grant bounds only what passes through the account's authorization.

The proof boundary

The theorems are about the Lean translation of the Rust, not the compiled Wasm, and nothing checks one against the other. The specification is trusted. The account code around the function is tested, and no single theorem covers the whole authorization path.

Against a grantee

A key named by several rules holds the union of their authority, and caps are per rule, not per grant. Each call is checked alone (the game-grant diagram). A standing token allowance and the owner's execute move value outside any grant, and a grant rule on a token contract with no function list also allows approve. Replacing a capped rule starts an empty window, so lowering a cap right after the grantee spent up to it allows the new limit again in the same window. A window holds at most 907 spends.

Against a relayer

Delegated signers do not bind rule IDs. A rule edited in place between signing and inclusion decides under its new contents. A refused entry can be resubmitted until it expires. Grant installs take no expected version, so two pre-signed installs for one grant can land in either order.

Beyond these

Documents are capped at 8 signers, 10 rules and 8,192 canonical bytes, so a grant beyond those caps cannot be installed. No external audit has been done. Theft of the owner's key, recovery and the components the account trusts are discussed in the vision paper.

11Related work

Grants borrow from capability systems, account abstraction and policy languages; what differs is where a grant lives and what keeps its state.

Capabilities. Hardy's confused deputy [14] motivates designating authority explicitly, as capability systems do. SPKI [15], macaroons [16], Biscuit [17] and UCAN [18] attenuate tokens that a holder carries. A Perch grant is state installed in the account, and a windowed cap needs a record of past spending that a bearer token cannot carry alone. In Schneider's terms [19], a cap makes the evaluator an execution monitor whose state is the execution's own history.

Account abstraction. On EVM chains, ERC-4337 [20] accounts validate each operation, and three drafts, ERC-7579 [21] modules, ERC-7710 [22] delegations and ERC-7715 [9] permission requests, cover similar ground. Zodiac Roles [23] is close in shape to a Perch grant. MetaMask's delegation framework [24] caps transfers per fixed period, resetting at each period's start (ERC20PeriodTransferEnforcer.sol:11-16), so spends either side of a reset can reach twice the cap within one period's length; from empty records, a Perch window is bounded wherever it is placed (Theorem 6).

Policy languages. Cedar [25] pairs a policy language with formal models; its verification-guided development [26] links a Lean model to Rust by differential testing, where Perch proves a translation of the Rust. In XACML's terms [27], Perch's decision point is linked into the account. Synthesising a predicate from a recording, as oz-policy-builder does, is related to policy mining [28].

Tools. Certora Sunbeam [29] checks specifications against compiled Wasm, and Komet [30] fuzzes and symbolically executes it; both complement a proof about a pure function. soroban-sim [10] runs real contract bytes in a portable host, which is what lets this page run both designs.

12What next

Proposed, not built: an agent runtime with delegated budgets, installable attenuated grants, exact entry roots, and a recording front end that writes grant documents.

In the proposed runtime, a root agent holds a Perch grant and hands child agents budgets from a delegation tree, where spending beneath any node stays within its allowance (Theorem 7, a model result). An account entry point would install the attenuated grants that §03 models. Exact authorization-entry roots are in draft (Perch #69 and rule-core #17). We also propose Cedar as an authoring language and a measurement of both designs with exact metering. The vision paper describes each.

13What this shows

A Perch grant is a bounded permission that the account decides at one point, with its spending window charged in the same decision. The decision function's Lean translation is proved to accept only what the rules allow, to keep each window within its limit, and to allow no call to the account or its adopted recovery contracts; the code around it is tested, and nothing is audited. soroban-sim runs Perch and an OpenZeppelin account with oz-policy-builder from their real contract bytes in this page. In the recorded run, their verdicts differed only where the owner removed the limit over time. oz-policy-builder is ahead in maturity, authoring and expressiveness, and Perch should learn from it. What remains is merging the design, an external audit, and measurement with exact metering.

The Perch account is built with its committed infrastructure pins rewritten to the compiler, interpreter and spending-limit builds of the same run, so its bytes differ from a committed-pin build and from the testnet preview's. The three OpenZeppelin-side hashes equal oz-policy-builder's testnet records; built with stellar-cli 27.0.0, the account and spending_limit match after normalize-oz.py sets the CLI version string in their metadata to 27.1.0's. sim/README.md beside the page, and the vision paper's reproducibility record, list these hashes and a registry stub the page does not load.

Rebuilding the simulation. The simulation page carries its build sources. Their BUILD.md fetches the pinned trees by commit and rebuilds the contracts, the driver (native and WASI), the recorded run and the page; sim/README.md beside the page gives the commands.

Reproducing the proofs. In rule-core, with Charon 7b462d9, Aeneas ab34bd2 and Lean v4.35.0: run tools/extract.sh --check, then lake build in formal/. rule-core's README gives the full commands.

This page and its PDF are built from the same outline, facts, tables and figures. The earlier Perch-only experiment and its full reproducibility record are in the vision paper.
