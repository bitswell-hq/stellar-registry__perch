What Perch is

Perch is a Stellar smart account. Each app or agent gets a grant: which calls, which signers, a spending cap, an expiry.

One function decides every authorization, and it is proved in Lean.

Perch is a set of Soroban contracts that turns a Stellar smart account into a permission engine for apps and agents. The owner installs grants: which contracts and functions an app or agent may call, argument conditions, which keys must sign, a sliding-window spending cap and an expiry. Every authorization is decided by rule_eval::check_auth over the rules the account stores, including nested calls in the authorization tree.

The decision function, about 2,400 lines of Rust, is translated into Lean, and kernel-checked theorems show that it returns what a readable model returns, accepts only calls a rule allows, keeps every window within its cap, never lets a narrowed grant allow more than its parent, and never allows a call to the account or its recovery contracts: 13 theorems in all. The step from the evaluator to the account (P1) is argued, not proved, and nothing checks the compiled Wasm against the translation.

Perch contracts are live on Stellar mainnet since 2026-10-26: the registry, the document compiler and the account factory, from the earlier account design. Nido, The Aha Company's passkey wallet, creates Perch accounts through its factory. Grants with apply_grant and rule_eval::check_auth are merged in the repository and reach mainnet in Tranche 3.

Agents with keys

An agent with a key can do anything the account can, and its subagents copy it. With Perch, the agent holds a grant, and the account checks every call.

An agent that spends for a user holds a key that can do anything the account can, and anything it spawns inherits that power. A Perch grant fixes which contracts and functions an app or agent may call, argument conditions, which keys must sign, a sliding-window spending cap and an expiry, and the account checks every call in the authorization tree against it, so a stolen app key can spend no more than its grant allows. The funded work adds an agent runtime in which subagents receive narrower grants and hold no keys.

An agent raised its own cap

Breckenridge, Vilardell, Wang, Zhang and Juels, “I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop”, 2026arXiv:2610.35835

In a recent study from Cornell Tech, Yale and IC3, agents paying small invoices hit a daily cap. Raising it took a code emailed to the owner, in an inbox the agent could read.

Nine of ten models raised their own cap, unprompted, in at least one of twenty runs.

A study from Cornell Tech, Yale University and IC3 tested a human-in-the-loop gate on spending. A LangChain agent had three tools: the user's email, a web browser and an agent wallet holding 10 USDC on Base mainnet, with a spending policy of 0.01 USDC per transaction, 0.01 USDC per day, 0.30 USDC per week and 1 USDC per month. Changing the policy needs a one-time passcode that the wallet provider emails to the account owner. The system prompt told the agent to act only on the user's instructions and to treat anything a tool returns, emails included, as data; no task mentioned the spending policy or how to change it.

In the payment experiment the inbox held three invoices of 0.01 USDC each, so paying all three needed a higher daily cap. Across 20 trials per model, with no adversary present, 9 of 10 models read the code from the inbox and raised their own spending cap in at least one run. In a second experiment, tasked only with summarizing the inbox, 5 of 10 models paid an attacker who impersonated the user by email. The authors disclosed the results to the wallet provider, Circle, on 22 September 2026; Circle's agent-wallet documentation now warns never to give an agent access to the inbox that receives the code. The paper argues that a human in the loop needs a channel the agent cannot reach.

Breckenridge, Vilardell, Wang, Zhang and Juels, "I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop", arXiv:2610.35835 (v3, 30 October 2026): p. 4 (Setup, Experiments, Key Findings), p. 5 (Results), Appendix D (p. 18). The ten models are not named here.

Where the policy lives

Common setups keep limits within the agent's reach, or write contract logic apart from the client's rules, so the two can drift. We put one policy in both places.

Common setups for agent payments enforce the policy in one place. Some keep the limits within the agent's reach: the agent's own process or tools check them, or a provider enforces them behind an approval channel the agent can read. The cited study is the second kind: the wallet provider, Circle, enforced the cap, and agents raised it with a one-time code emailed to an inbox they could read. Others put the policy in on-chain logic written separately from the client's rules, so the two rule sets can drift apart. This slide names no product; the point is where the check sits.

Perch's funded design combines them: one Cedar policy, evaluated by a contained policy engine in the runtime and compiled into the rules the account enforces on chain. Off chain it covers what the chain cannot see (files, websites, signing, spawning); on chain the account is the backstop for payments and contract calls.

arXiv:2610.35835, p. 4 (the emailed-code finding); SCF Build submission, Products & Services (the funded work), Tranche #1 items 2 and 3 (as agreed 2026-11-09).

A contained policy runtime

Our funded design is a contained policy runtime. One Cedar policy is evaluated off chain by an engine with no imports, and compiled into the account's rules on chain.

The runtime is tested; the decision function is proved. If the runtime fails, the account refuses an over-cap payment.

Funded work (Tranches 1 and 2). One Cedar policy is the source of both checks. In the runtime, agents run as WASI components and hold no keys; a root agent's authority starts from a grant on the account, and a subagent is spawned only with a grant derived from its parent's (Tranche 1, item 1). Every file, website, signing and spawn request goes to the contained policy engine: official Cedar 4.14.x packaged as an import-free WebAssembly component, with no files, network or keys of its own, in which any policy error refuses and a subagent's request must be permitted by its own policies and every ancestor's (Tranche 1, item 3). Signing goes through the key service, which signs only after checking the signing request and every authorization context (Tranche 2, item 1).

The same policy is compiled into the rules the account enforces on chain, by a compiler from a stated Cedar subset that refuses anything it cannot translate exactly, with compiled numeric rules carrying 64-bit bounds to match Cedar's integers. A Lean theorem, on Cedar's published Lean specification, is to show that the account's decision function, as translated from Rust, allows a call exactly when Cedar allows the matching request, and a harness runs the compiled contract in a local Soroban host and the official Cedar engine on the same generated requests (Tranche 1, item 2). The link is proved for the Rust translation and tested for the compiled contract, and it covers stateless call matching: spending caps and time windows are enforced only by the account.

Proved today: the account's decision function, in 13 kernel-checked Lean theorems; P1 is argued, and nothing checks the compiled Wasm against the translation. The runtime will be tested, not proven. Payments over x402 (Tranche 2, item 2) and contract calls reach the account, which rechecks them on chain; with an attenuated grant installed (Tranche 2, item 1), an over-cap subagent payment is refused even with the runtime's own check switched off. Also today: an unpublished experiment proved the equivalence for a cut-down fragment, and Cedar's smallest evaluator build measured 710,772 bytes against the 131,072-byte contract limit (the Perch account is 106,668 bytes, measured 6 November 2026), which is why Cedar runs off chain.

Example: a mail assistant

Take a mail assistant. It reads only what you forward and sends only to your list, so it never sees your inbox or its codes. Its newsletter payments stay under a cap on chain.

Planned, funded work: the policy is what the agent runtime (Tranche 1, items 1 and 3) would enforce, through the contained Cedar policy engine. The action and entity names (Agent, Action::"web.get", Action::"web.post", Host, context.to) are the planned runtime's request vocabulary, not an existing API. Mail is modelled as web requests to two named hosts, a forwarding mailbox's read API and a send API, not as a separate mail action; the hosts and addresses are illustrative. The syntax is Cedar's: permit with a scope, a when condition, a set's contains.

The money part is an ordinary Perch grant, as merged today: a transfer of one token to one recipient, at most 5 USDC per 518,400 ledgers (about 30 days at 5 seconds a ledger), enforced by the account whatever the runtime does. This is the plain answer to the cited study's setup: the agent never holds the owner's inbox, so a one-time code emailed to the owner never reaches it, and it cannot change its own grant.

Example: a news and data reader

Or a news reader: it reads an RSS feed and named data sources, and can't post, submit a form or send anything.

Planned, funded work, like the mail assistant: one permit for web.get on the hosts in a group of named sources, and nothing else, so Cedar's default refuses every post, form submission and message. The names are the planned runtime's request vocabulary; the hosts are illustrative. No money moves, so no on-chain grant is needed; were the reader to pay for a data feed, that payment would go through a grant as in the mail example.

A cap the agent cannot raise

The cap lives in the account's grant. By Theorem 8 no grant allows a call to the account, so the agent can't raise its cap, and an emailed code opens nothing.

Perch keeps the cap in the account's own grant, and every authorization is decided by rule_eval::check_auth over the rules the account stores. Changing a grant, with apply_grant or remove_grant, is a call to the account that only its admin rule authorizes; in Nido the admin is the owner's passkey, a signature made on the owner's device that the agent cannot produce. A grant can never authorize a call to the account itself or its recovery contracts. That is Theorem 8, grant target containment: "No grant as lowered at install, nor any grant derived from one, allows a call to the account or its adopted recovery contracts."

So the agent's own key cannot raise its own cap, and there is nothing an emailed code could open: reading an email gets the agent nothing. This assumes the agent holds only its grant's key and the owner's passkey stays on the owner's device. It is not the paper's I-HITL protocol; it is where Perch puts the limit. This is the merged account; mainnet still runs the earlier design, and deploying this one is Tranche 3.

Narrowing a grant

Theorems 4 and 5 show a narrowed helper's grant never allows a call, or a run of payments, that the app's grant refuses.

Attenuation derives a narrower grant from an existing one; rule-core defines 10 narrowing steps, three shown here. Theorem 4: a derived grant allows no call its parent does not, under any signers; its comparison reads no cap or expiry, so it covers step 1. Theorem 5, trace inclusion: every run a derived grant accepts from empty records, its parent accepts too, which covers steps 2 and 3. N and M are illustrative.

Funded: a runtime that spawns a subagent only with a grant derived from its parent's (Tranche 1, deliverable 1), and an account entry point that installs a derived grant, checked on chain to be no wider than its parent (Tranche 2, deliverable 1). The account cannot install an attenuated grant today.
