Perch Agent Grants, an Open Track submission for an SCF Build Award by The Aha Company. Perch is a set of Soroban contracts that turns a Stellar smart account into a permission engine for apps and agents; this award funds an agent runtime whose root authority is a grant on a Perch account. Solid cyan is what exists today; dashed magenta, with printer's marks, is the work the award funds.

SCF Build submission, Submission Title, Build Award Track and Products & Services. The dial is the Perch deck's title drawing.

Agents with keys

An agent that spends for a user holds a key that can do anything the account can, and anything it spawns inherits that power. A Perch grant fixes which contracts and functions an app or agent may call, argument conditions, which keys must sign, a sliding-window spending cap and an expiry, and the account checks every call in the authorization tree against it, so a stolen app key can spend no more than its grant allows. The funded work adds an agent runtime in which subagents receive narrower grants and hold no keys.

An agent raised its own cap

Breckenridge, Vilardell, Wang, Zhang and Juels, “I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop”, 2026arXiv:2610.35835

A study from Cornell Tech, Yale University and IC3 tested a human-in-the-loop gate on spending. A LangChain agent had three tools: the user's email, a web browser and an agent wallet holding 10 USDC on Base mainnet, with a spending policy of 0.01 USDC per transaction, 0.01 USDC per day, 0.30 USDC per week and 1 USDC per month. Changing the policy needs a one-time passcode that the wallet provider emails to the account owner. The system prompt told the agent to act only on the user's instructions and to treat anything a tool returns, emails included, as data; no task mentioned the spending policy or how to change it.

In the payment experiment the inbox held three invoices of 0.01 USDC each, so paying all three needed a higher daily cap. Across 20 trials per model, with no adversary present, 9 of 10 models read the code from the inbox and raised their own spending cap in at least one run. In a second experiment, tasked only with summarizing the inbox, 5 of 10 models paid an attacker who impersonated the user by email. The authors disclosed the results to the wallet provider, Circle, on 22 September 2026; Circle's agent-wallet documentation now warns never to give an agent access to the inbox that receives the code. The paper argues that a human in the loop needs a channel the agent cannot reach.

Breckenridge, Vilardell, Wang, Zhang and Juels, "I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop", arXiv:2610.35835 (v3, 30 October 2026): p. 4 (Setup, Experiments, Key Findings), p. 5 (Results), Appendix D (p. 18). The ten models are not named here.

A cap the agent cannot raise

Perch keeps the cap in the account's own grant, and every authorization is decided by rule_eval::check_auth over the rules the account stores. Changing a grant, with apply_grant or remove_grant, is a call to the account that only its admin rule authorizes; in Nido the admin is the owner's passkey, a signature made on the owner's device that the agent cannot produce. A grant can never authorize a call to the account itself or its recovery contracts. That is Theorem 8, grant target containment: "No grant as lowered at install, nor any grant derived from one, allows a call to the account or its adopted recovery contracts."

So the agent's own key cannot raise its own cap, and there is nothing an emailed code could open: reading an email gets the agent nothing. This assumes the agent holds only its grant's key and the owner's passkey stays on the owner's device. It is not the paper's I-HITL protocol; it is where Perch puts the limit. This is the merged account; mainnet still runs the earlier design, and deploying this one is Tranche 3.

Perch contracts have been live on Stellar mainnet since 2026-10-26: the registry, the document compiler and the account factory, from the earlier design (source c135aca, channel beta, a single-key registry admin). Nido, The Aha Company's passkey wallet, creates Perch accounts on mainnet through its factory. Nido's wallet counts, the mainnet deployment and npm downloads are reported under Nido's SCF #41 award; they are context here, not claimed for this ask.

Merged in the repository and not funded here: the account with grants (apply_grant), every authorization decided by rule_eval::check_auth, recovery, and passkey and Ed25519 verifiers. The decision function is translated into Lean, and kernel-checked theorems show that it returns what a readable model returns, accepts only calls a rule allows, keeps every window within its cap, never lets a narrowed grant allow more than its parent, and never allows a call to the account or its recovery contracts. A merge is not a mainnet migration: deploying the account changes to mainnet is Tranche 3, deliverable 1.

Narrowing a grant

Attenuation derives a narrower grant from an existing one; rule-core defines 10 narrowing steps, three shown here. Theorem 4: a derived grant allows no call its parent does not, under any signers; its comparison reads no cap or expiry, so it covers step 1. Theorem 5, trace inclusion: every run a derived grant accepts from empty records, its parent accepts too, which covers steps 2 and 3. N and M are illustrative.

Funded: a runtime that spawns a subagent only with a grant derived from its parent's (Tranche 1, deliverable 1), and an account entry point that installs a derived grant, checked on chain to be no wider than its parent (Tranche 2, deliverable 1). The account cannot install an attenuated grant today.

The agent runtime

Funded work. Agents run as WASI components and hold no keys. A root agent's authority is a grant installed on a Perch account and read from it, and a subagent is spawned only with a grant derived from its parent's; a wider spawn is refused (Tranche 1, item 1). Every file, website, signing and spawn request goes to the Cedar policy engine, official Cedar packaged as an import-free WebAssembly component with no files, sockets or keys of its own; a subagent's request must be permitted by its own policies and every ancestor's (Tranche 1, item 3).

Signing goes through the key service, which signs only after checking the signing request and every authorization context (Tranche 2, item 1). Payments, over x402 on Stellar (Tranche 2, item 2), and contract calls reach the Perch account, which rechecks them on chain. With an attenuated grant installed on the account (Tranche 2, item 1), a subagent payment over its cap is refused by the account even with the runtime's own check switched off, so an over-cap payment is refused even if the runtime is compromised. Today: the account, grants, the proofs and Nido.

One policy, two engines

Funded (Tranche 1, deliverables 2 and 3, agreed 2026-11-09): a compiler from a stated Cedar subset to the rules the account enforces, which refuses anything it cannot translate exactly, with compiled numeric rules carrying 64-bit bounds to match Cedar's integers; a Lean theorem, on Cedar's published Lean specification, that the account's decision function, as translated from Rust, allows a call exactly when Cedar allows the matching request; a harness running the compiled contract in a local Soroban host and the official Cedar engine on the same generated requests, with zero disagreements; and the official Cedar 4.14.x policy engine as an import-free WebAssembly component in which any policy error refuses and a subagent's request must be permitted by its own policies and every ancestor's.

Today: measured on 6 November 2026, Cedar's smallest evaluator build is 710,772 bytes, the network's contract limit 131,072 bytes and the Perch account 106,668 bytes, which is why Cedar runs off chain; and an unpublished experiment proved the equivalence for a cut-down fragment, with no placeholder proofs. The policy drawn is illustrative.

Verified Cedar-to-Perch compiler and two-engine test harness, as on the previous slide. 3.
