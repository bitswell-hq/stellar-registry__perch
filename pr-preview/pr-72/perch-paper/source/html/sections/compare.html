oz-policy-builder adds rules to OpenZeppelin accounts through a shared on-chain interpreter; Perch decides inside the account. The designs differ in how a limit holds over time, how a rule is written, and what evidence stands behind each.

This section compares Perch with oz-policy-builder {{cite:oz-policy-builder}} at {{fact:opb-pin-short}} (release {{fact:opb-release}} and {{fact:opb-commits-after-release}} later commits, none under its contracts), on OpenZeppelin stellar-contracts {{fact:oz-v080-tag}}. Claims about its design are read from its source and public documentation at that pin. We also ran it: we built it and ran its test suites, ran one task through both designs, and ran one rule with the interpreter alone. Each claim says what it rests on: reading the source, a run, tests or a proof. These are different kinds of evidence about different objects, and the section does not rank one above another. Several words mean different things in the two systems (grant, scope, policy, interpreter, execute, recovery, cap, expiry); the glossary gives both meanings.

Two places to decide

oz-policy-builder has two halves. Off chain, a synthesiser ({{fact:opb-synth-package}}, with a command-line tool and an MCP server that expose {{fact:opb-tools-count}} tools) records a transaction or takes a declared Intent, writes a predicate in the interpreter's wire form, dry-runs it with check, renders a review, and returns an unsigned install transaction. On chain, one shared policy-interpreter contract, attached to an OpenZeppelin context rule as a policy, evaluates the stored predicate in enforce on every guarded call. Much of its docs/stellar.md describes Prime, a custody product built on these parts; the interpreter and the synthesiser are the parts that correspond to Perch's engine. Perch's deployed build {{fact:perch-deployed-source-short}} used this attached-policy shape on a fork of OpenZeppelin's account library; the design in this paper moves the decision into the account.

Three more ideas, from oz-policy-builder

  • Predicate. The interpreter's stored expression for one (account, rule) pair. It denies by default and has no not; its ne and nin deny when an operand does not resolve.
  • Execution adapter. A contract that runs a batch of calls atomically, because Soroban allows one host-function invocation per transaction. A predicate on its execute can relate the calls in that batch.
  • Keyless rule. A rule with no signer. The interpreter admits one when it is bound to an execution adapter; anyone may then run what the predicate permits. Perch refuses a rule with no signer (UnguardedRule).

A bound on each call, or a limit over time

oz-policy-builder's interpreter decides each call alone. Its documentation says so: it does not see what a transaction moved, keep rolling totals or count calls (docs/stellar.md:404-407). Its synthesiser writes a per-call bound on an amount (lte) and refuses an unbounded amount unless the owners override it. A limit over time comes from somewhere else: OpenZeppelin's spending_limit, an optional second policy that oz-policy-builder attaches on transfer only, or a token allowance and its expiry.

We ran the per-call bound alone. On a local network, an OpenZeppelin account whose rule carried only the interpreter, with a per-call bound of {{fact:opb-per-call-bound-xlm}} XLM, applied {{fact:opb-per-call-applied-sends}} sends that moved {{fact:opb-per-call-moved-xlm}} XLM in all, and refused one of {{fact:opb-per-call-bound-xlm}} XLM and a stroop. This is the documented behaviour. With spending_limit attached, as in the walkthrough, the second payment in one window is refused there, with SpendingLimitExceeded ({{fact:oz-spending-limit-exceeded}}).

In Perch the window is part of the grant's rule. The evaluator charges it in the same decision that checks the call, and T6 proves that, from empty spending records, the charges an accepted run makes to a capped rule stay within its limit in every window. A Perch document cannot bound a single call's amount; the window limit is its only amount bound ({{ref:sec:grants}}).

{{ref:fig:fig-two-paths}} follows one guarded call through both accounts, phase by phase. Perch makes its whole decision, caps included, before any signer is authenticated, and writes the spending record in the account. The OpenZeppelin account selects one rule per context, refuses a signer that is on no selected rule, and authenticates the signers. It then runs each attached policy's enforce in the rule's stored order: address order for the policies the rule was created with, then any added later (OpenZeppelin storage.rs:830, 1304). Over the cap, Perch refuses in its decision with auth error {{fact:auth-error-rule-eval-refused}}, and the OpenZeppelin account refuses in spending_limit. The figure is read from source.

Recording a call, or declaring a grant

oz-policy-builder starts from what an app did. Its synthesiser takes a recorded transaction, or a declared Intent, and writes the least predicate that permits it. It verifies the result by dry-running the deployed interpreter's own check, renders a review for the owner, and returns unsigned install bytes. A recording shows no total, expiry or other rule; oz-policy-builder takes the first two as install inputs (spendingLimit, validUntilLedger) and reads every live rule of the account in its overlap scan.

Perch starts from what the owner grants. The wallet declares a grant document; the account's document compiler validates it, and the account lowers it against its own authority, so that no grant rule can target the account or its excluded set ({{ref:sec:grants}}). The two approaches are complementary: one finds the least permission for an observed action, the other states a permission and checks it against the account. We propose a recording front end for Perch that would emit a grant document ({{ref:sec:agents}}).

{{ref:fig:fig-two-spellings}} writes one intent both ways: the game grant that Nido's testnet preview installed, not the demo's grant 3. It pins the recipient, and it has a {{fact:preview-game-period-ledgers}}-ledger window and an expiry. The numbered list matches the marks in the figure.

{{fig:fig-two-spellings}}
  1. Both systems select one rule per context, and each rule sees its own call alone (oz-policy-builder's docs/architecture.md:307-309; {{ref:fig:fig-contexts}}, case c). So in both, a transfer to the game is allowed without a guess.
  2. In Perch the window cap is part of the rule. oz-policy-builder turns the Intent's cap into a per-call lte and attaches spending_limit for the window. A Perch document cannot bound a single call's amount.
  3. Perch installs both rules in one transaction. oz-policy-builder builds one add_context_rule per install_policy call (read from source; this example's installs were not run).
  4. Perch's grant rule holds a passkey as an external signer. oz-policy-builder writes every rule signer as Delegated, and the interpreter refuses External signers (error {{fact:opb-error-external-signer}}), so an oz-policy-builder app signs as a Stellar or contract address. Its install is authorized by sourceAccount, a G… account that must be a signer of rule 0, the owners' context rule through which rules are added. A Perch install needs the owner's authorization in the account's entry, and any account may submit it.
  5. Expiry: {{fact:perch-expiry-semantics}}. OpenZeppelin's valid_until authorizes through itself, so validUntilLedger: N − 1 names the same last ledger.
  6. oz-policy-builder can consult live state, for example a read of a registry before permitting a recipient; a Perch rule reads nothing outside the account. A Perch grant ends when the account recovers; oz-policy-builder has no account-key recovery, and the “recovery” of its Prime product moves custody funds to a fixed address.

What evidence stands behind each

oz-policy-builder. We built it at {{fact:opb-pin-short}}; its {{fact:opb-rust-test-runs}} Rust test runs and {{fact:opb-ts-tests}} TypeScript tests pass. We ran its record, synthesise, verify and install flow against an OpenZeppelin {{fact:oz-v080-tag}} account on {{fact:opb-local-network}}, and the calls outside the rule that we sent were refused with the codes its documentation gives. A synthesised policy is verified by simulating the deployed interpreter's own check, so one evaluator both verifies and enforces. Its {{fact:opb-interpreter-tests}} interpreter tests include {{fact:opb-tests-against-oz08}} against OpenZeppelin's {{fact:oz-v080-tag}} account, and a worst-case suite runs the interpreter's Wasm. It keeps a STRIDE threat register with a control and a status for each threat. An external audit of {{fact:opb-release}} has been {{fact:opb-audit-status}} since {{fact:opb-audit-start}}, with the OpenZeppelin account and policies outside its scope, and its off-chain packages were hardened after an external penetration test (pull request {{fact:opb-pentest-pr}}, branch {{fact:opb-pentest-branch}}).

Perch. The evaluator is proved in Lean over its Aeneas translation ({{ref:sec:proofs}}). The translation is tied to the Rust by CI, which regenerates and diffs it, and the account is tied to the theorems by {{fact:lean-doc-traces}} Lean-generated traces run through the account shell. The theorems are about the translation, not the Wasm; the shell is tested, and its Wasm runs in Perch's release-stack tests, the reference run and the testnet preview. No external audit has been completed.

oz-policy-builder pins an interpreter on testnet and none on mainnet; the OpenZeppelin {{fact:oz-v072-tag}} policies are on mainnet. This paper's design runs on testnet as a preview, and Perch's earlier design is on mainnet ({{ref:sec:status}}).

Several calls in one authorization

OpenZeppelin judges each context under its own rule: a call that authorizes two contexts needs a rule for each, because the interpreter sees one authorized call at a time (oz-policy-builder's docs/architecture.md:307-309). oz-policy-builder's answer is the execution adapter. A predicate on the adapter's execute can relate the calls of one batch, and a rule bound to the adapter is honoured only for a call in a batch the adapter checked (docs/stellar.md:191-195). Perch also checks each context alone ({{ref:fig:fig-contexts}}, case c). Draft changes after the pinned build ({{fact:exact-roots-prs}}) let a rule require its entry's root, with no theorem yet. Here the designs are alike, apart from the adapter.

What takes part in a decision

An OpenZeppelin account composes a decision from several contracts: the interpreter; a threshold policy if more than one signer must approve; spending_limit for a window; and, in oz-policy-builder's Prime custody setting, the execution adapter and a custody gate. With the interpreter alone, any one authenticated signer of a rule suffices, because thresholds are separate policies; oz-policy-builder's builder refuses a several-signer rule without one unless the owners override it (policy-synth README, “Threshold”). A Perch grant is one document, and one evaluator linked into the account decides it, threshold and window included; in authorizing a grant's call, the account calls out only to verify signatures.

The walkthrough's traces show the contracts each design called for each payment ({{ref:fig:fig-same-task}}): the OpenZeppelin account called spending_limit and the interpreter, and its delegated signer approved in a second authorization entry; Perch called its verifier. The signer kinds differ, because oz-policy-builder takes only delegated signers, so the call structure reflects that as well as the designs. These are recorded call structures, not costs.

Changing and ending a grant

oz-policy-builder's interpreter pins a policed rule to its exact signer set: install stores a hash of the set, and enforce refuses with error {{fact:opb-error-signers-changed}} once it differs, so changing who signs means adding a new rule and removing the old one. Rules are removed one at a time. Removing a rule with only rule 0's signatures leaves the interpreter's entry for it until the entry archives, because the interpreter's uninstall needs the policy admins' own authorization; the account never reads that entry again (docs/stellar.md:548-562). In Perch the owner removes a grant with one call, and a completed recovery advances the grant generation, after which no earlier grant authorizes through the account (T10, an account-model result; tested, and executed in the reference run).

What a signature binds

OpenZeppelin {{fact:oz-v080-tag}} binds the account and the selected rule IDs for both signer kinds: an external signer signs the hash of its AuthDigestPreimage, and a delegated signer authorizes that preimage through require_auth_for_args. Its {{fact:oz-v072-tag}} line does not bind the account for an external signer with a legacy Address credential. Perch binds the account and the rule IDs for an external signer and the account alone for a delegated one ({{ref:tab:tab-binding}}). In the walkthrough, a key that signs in the app's name without being the app's key fails the host's signature check in both, with {{fact:verifier-trap-error}}: on the OpenZeppelin side for the delegated Stellar account's entry, before any policy runs; on the Perch side when the verifier traps, after the evaluator accepts.

What this comparison does not show

  • Design claims about oz-policy-builder are read from source at the pins; our runs cover its test suites, its install flow, the per-call run above, and the one task of {{ref:tab:tab-walkthrough}}.
  • The walkthrough ran one task, in one harness, with different signer kinds on the two sides. It is not a cost comparison: fees, memory, storage and exact-metered costs on both designs, over more workloads, are proposed ({{ref:sec:agents}}).
